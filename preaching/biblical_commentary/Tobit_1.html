Father Thomas O'Donnell

Commentary on Tobit

Tobit 1

[1–2] Tribe of Napthali ... taken into captivityem> Tobit is a man living in exile. Bede reminds us that this symbolizes our own spiritual exile, when we were captured by the prince of this world. Despite the Christian influences on our Western culture, do we not still find ourselves in exile today?
[3] walked in the ways of truth and righteousness ... performed many acts of charity This is a book about a man whose live exemplifies the two-fold command of love of God and neighbor. How am I supposed to live while in exile? The answer is the same for everyone.
[4–8]The whole tribe of Napthali ... deserted the house of Jerusalem ... where all the tribes should sacrifice ... All the tribes that joined in apostasy used to sacrifice to the calf Baal, and so did the house of Naphtali my forefather ... I alone went often to Jerusalem for the feasts ... The tribes have abandoned God, are in the power of a foreign/evil prince, and sacrifice to a false god. The first "half" of the Decalogue (the Ten Words or Ten Commandments) is violated: love of God. They are loving something (or someone) more than the one God, to whom our lives must be wholly directed. Tobit's heart, however, remains firmly set on the one God and so fulfills the first half of the Decalogue.

Tobit 14

Benedictus es, Domine, Deus universi,
quia de tua largitate accepimus vinum,
quod tibi offerimus,
fructum vitis et operis manuum hominum,
ex quo nobis fiet potus spiritalis.
The kingdom of heaven is like a landowner
who went out at dawn to hire laborers for his vineyard [...] (Matthew 20:1, the Gospel for Year A, 25th Sunday of Ordinary Time)

Making wine is a remarkably simple process that involves tremendous care, skill, and labor. I mean simple in the original sense: a real wine involves only one ingredient, grapes. Countless grapes, cultivated carefully by the farmer; soil, water, and sunshine. There aren’t 72 ingredients on the back of the box. The grapes grow on their vines and in the autumn the owner of the vineyard begins the harvest with the help of his laborers. The grapes cannot become wine until they are crushed and fermented. It’s a tremendously labor-intensive process, especially before the advent of modern machinery. But even today, the craft of wine-making still involves no small amount of human effort and attention.

The preparatory prayer over the wine beautifully emphasizes the goodness of God from whom all things come. Through God’s bounty we have received everything. There’s nothing we can offer back to God that we haven’t first received from him. My existence and the existence of everything around me is part of God’s plan of infinite love. He created us out of nothing simply so that he could love us. That’s why he brought each one of us into the world.

The preparatory prayer also acknowledges that the wine we offer God comes through the work of human hands. Human labor is essential. It’s part of this offering made back to God. He wants human labor to be a part of this offering. Even if one is not a vineyard owner or a laborer in an actual vineyard, every person has daily labors. Not simply one’s paid career, important as that is, but one’s ordinary home life, married life, and family life. The many ways in which one seeks to care for neighbors of all kinds: children, siblings, parents, spouse, the poor, sick, and elderly. These are labors. And the most difficult labors often go unacknowledged. People rearrange their days, their years, their career aspirations, in order to labor on behalf of another. We labor through disappointment and misunderstandings and miscommunications, difficulties, and challenging diagnoses.

The supreme and eternal Priest, Christ Jesus, since he wills to continue his witness and service also through the laity, vivifies them in this Spirit and increasingly urges them on to every good and perfect work.

For besides intimately linking them to His life and His mission, He also gives them a sharing in His priestly function of offering spiritual worship for the glory of God and the salvation of men. For this reason the laity, dedicated to Christ and anointed by the Holy Spirit, are marvelously called and wonderfully prepared so that ever more abundant fruits of the Spirit may be produced in them. For all their works, prayers and apostolic endeavors, their ordinary married and family life, their daily occupations, their physical and mental relaxation, if carried out in the Spirit, and even the hardships of life, if patiently borne—all these become “spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ” (1 Peter 2:5). Together with the offering of the Lord's body, they are most fittingly offered in the celebration of the Eucharist. Thus, as those everywhere who adore in holy activity, the laity consecrate the world itself to God. (Lumen gentium 34)

Our life is a labor in a vineyard. If and only if we allow the Lord to bear great fruit in that labor.

God’s standard for judging our daily labors is so different from the standard of the world. It seems so obvious that God’s standards are different from the world’s standard. Because if God’s standard for judging human labors were the same as the world’s standard, then the list of canonized saints would be identical to the list of the world’s wealthiest people. Or perhaps the world’s most beautiful people. Or the people with the most followers on social media. And we know that these lists—the list of canonized saints and the list of the world’s most successful—are simply not the same. But how easy is it to fall into the temptation to forget this once we pass through the doors of the church building! We so quickly begin to measure the success of our labors by the standard of the world, and think that if I am not making enough money, or if I am not seen as attractive, or if I don’t have a huge public influence over others, then somehow I am failing.

For my thoughts are not your thoughts,
nor are your ways my ways, says the LORD. (Isaiah 55:8, the first reading for Year A, 25th Sunday of Ordinary Time)

God, in contrast, cares most importantly only that the Holy Spirit infuses all of our daily labors. That’s God’s main standard and really his only standard. With the Holy Spirit in our souls, filling and motivating and guiding us, our daily labors become the best harvest imaginable to God. They become fruitful not by the world’s standard of success but by God’s. The fruitfulness in which God delights is often found in the most hidden of places. The difficulties, sufferings, challenges of life, when infused with the Holy Spirit, become rich harvest for God.

If I go on living in the flesh,
that means fruitful labor for me. (Philippians 1:22, the second reading for Year A, 25th Sunday of Ordinary Time)*

The Holy Spirit can never transform an evil work into a good work. But the Holy Spirit can take the smallest and seemingly insignificant of our daily labors, the least successful labors in worldly terms, and even our failures (small and great), and totally transform them. With the Holy Spirit, who is divine love himself, infusing these labors, they become more fruitful than anything the world could imagine. Where the world sees failure, ugliness, suffering, and barrenness, the Holy Spirit has the power to infuse the harvest with overflowing love and make a rich crop for God the Father.

This is not because our self-sufficient and fallen human effort makes us Jesus’ favorite laborers. But because the Father and the Son pouring the Holy Spirit into our souls makes our labors produce choice wine for God’s kingdom. The grapes are harvested when ripe, crushed in the hardships of life, patiently fermented and stored so that our wine of charity can be offered back to the Father, united to the sacrifice of Christ on the cross. God then transforms the work of human hands into the very body and blood of his Son, for the salvation of the world.

Pope Benedict XVI wrote in Sacramentum caritatis (drawing on the Propositio of the Synod of Bishops on the Eucharist in 2005):

[The presentation of the gifts] is not to be viewed simply as a kind of “interval” between the liturgy of the word and the liturgy of the Eucharist. To do so would tend to weaken, at the least, the sense of a single rite made up of two interrelated parts. This humble and simple gesture is actually very significant: in the bread and wine that we bring to the altar, all creation is taken up by Christ the Redeemer to be transformed and presented to the Father. In this way we also bring to the altar all the pain and suffering of the world, in the certainty that everything has value in God's eyes. The authentic meaning of this gesture can be clearly expressed without the need for undue emphasis or complexity. It enables us to appreciate how God invites man to participate in bringing to fulfilment his handiwork, and in so doing, gives human labour its authentic meaning, since, through the celebration of the Eucharist, it is united to the redemptive sacrifice of Christ. (Sacramentum caritatis 47)

Every time we participate in the Mass, we must be willing to offer up our lives on the altar. Our labors in the vineyard of life, when they are a fruit of the Spirit, are taken up into God’s kingdom to be totally transformed. We must reject the lie that the small labors (in worldly terms) of our life are somehow not helpful or unproductive and unfruitful. Rather, in the constant practice of Christian penance by which we turn from sin and welcome the Holy Spirit into our soul, everything in our life can be taken up into the sacrifice of Christ by the power of the Holy Spirit, offered as a pleasing fragrance to God. The reward of such labor in the Spirit we receive back as our denarius, our daily wage (cf. Matthew 20:2), our daily bread: the body and blood of Christ, who is the kingdom itself.

From Sext and None in the Liturgia Horarum Week I, Sunday:

Do not be deceived: God is not mocked, for whatever one sows, that will he also reap. For the one who sows to his own flesh will from the flesh reap corruption, but the one who sows to the Spirit will from the Spirit reap eternal life. And let us not grow weary of doing good, for in due season we will reap, if we do not give up. So then, as we have opportunity, let us do good to everyone, and especially to those who are of the household of faith. (Galatian 6:7–10, ESV)*

See also Pope Francis' General Audience of February 28, 2018.

*Paul uses "flesh" in various ways. It sometimes refers simply to a Christian's earthly life, prior to death, and sometimes refers to a sinful life.
