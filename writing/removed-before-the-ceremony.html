Father Thomas O'Donnell

Empyting the tabernacle

9 September 2026

We often return to debates about the placement of the tabernacle in churches. Today, I think most Massgoers and regular adorers of the Blessed Sacrament would prefer to have the Blessed Sacrament reserved in a central place in the sanctuary. They wonder why and how Our Lord's Eucharistic presence was moved to a side chapel, especially throughout the 1960s and up through at least the 1990s in many Catholic churches in the United States.

There is much to be said on the history of the reservation of the Eucharist and the placement of the tabernacle. But one point of liturgical history which I don't think is cited enough regards the preparatory instructions for a Pontifical High Mass. I'm not saying it's never mentioned, but still is often forgotten.

In Adrian Fortescue's The Ceremonies of the Roman Rite Described, first published in 1917 (and the edition I can easily access online is from 1920), it says that for a Pontifical High Mass at the Throne, "If the Sanctissimum is reserved on the High Altar of the church, it should be removed before the ceremony" (p. 163). Likewise, for a Pontifical High Mass at the Faldstool (p. 183). And for a Pontifical High Mass for the Dead (p. 193).

This is not a very obscure reference since it has been readily available since 2013 in the EWTN archives of the Zenit Daily Dispatch, where Father McNamara's column always provides helpful context and commentary. I'm sure there are some other places where it is noted.

There are many things which Fortescue describes which are not done today for an episcopal Mass in the reformed Roman Rite. So there is not a one-to-one comparison. But my point is this: the practice of not having the Blessed Sacrament on the High Altar for (at least some) Masses didhave an immediate pre-Vatican-II theological foundation. (Which was not motivated by a lack of belief in the Real Presence.) And simultaneously, the lived experience of many Catholics as to how, where, and why the Blessed Sacrament was moved to side locations in many churches was very detrimental to our understanding not only of the Real Presence but also of the importance of regular and full participation in the Mass and worthy, fruitful reception of Our Lord in Holy Communion. In any debate about liturgical history and practice, it's important to maintain charity, patience, and an open historical perspective on all sides.
